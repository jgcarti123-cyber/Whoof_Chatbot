# Limping and Lameness in Dogs

## Assessing severity: the first question

The single most important distinction is **weight-bearing vs. non-weight-bearing lameness.**

- **Weight-bearing lameness:** The dog uses the limb but favours it — less weight on that leg, shortened stride, or a head nod when the affected leg hits the ground. Often less urgent.
- **Non-weight-bearing lameness:** The dog holds the limb completely off the ground. Treat as more serious; see a vet same day or next day unless an obvious superficial cause (e.g. thorn in paw) is found and removed.

## Common causes by location

**Paw/lower limb:**
- Thorn, glass, or foreign body in the pad or between toes — check carefully
- Cracked or cut paw pads
- Nail broken at the quick or overly long nail
- Interdigital cyst (swollen, red lump between toes — common in Labradors, Bulldogs)

**Upper limb / joint:**
- Muscle strain or sprain (often after vigorous play; sudden onset, improves with rest over 2-5 days)
- Arthritis/osteoarthritis (older dogs; worse after rest, improves briefly with gentle movement, worsens with overexertion)
- Hip dysplasia (large breeds — bunny-hopping gait, reluctance to climb stairs)
- Elbow dysplasia (young large-breed dogs — intermittent foreleg lameness)
- Luxating patella (knee cap slips — common in small breeds, intermittent skipping gait)
- Cruciate ligament rupture (sudden non-weight-bearing hind-limb lameness, often without a specific trauma event — requires surgery)

**Growth-related (puppies/young dogs):**
- Panosteitis — shifting lameness in large-breed puppies 5-18 months; self-resolving

## What to do at home first

1. Check the paw carefully for thorns, debris, cuts, or swelling between the toes.
2. If you find a small embedded object, remove with tweezers and clean with dilute povidone-iodine. Watch for infection.
3. Rest the dog: short lead walks only, no running, stairs, or jumping for 48-72 hours.
4. Apply a cold compress (wrapped ice pack) for 10-15 minutes 2-3× daily in the first 24-48 hours to reduce swelling.

## When to see a vet

- Non-weight-bearing lameness that persists more than a few hours
- Any lameness after a trauma (hit by car, fall, fight)
- Visible bone, joint out of place, or obvious deformity
- Rapid swelling of the joint or limb
- Lameness in a puppy (growth-plate injuries need prompt diagnosis)
- No improvement after 48-72 hours of rest
- Limping with fever, lethargy, or loss of appetite (consider joint infection or tick-borne disease)

## Pain relief: do not give human NSAIDs

Do NOT give ibuprofen (Nurofen, Advil), aspirin, or paracetamol/acetaminophen (Crocin, Tylenol) to dogs. These are toxic to dogs and can cause gastric ulcers, kidney failure, or liver failure. If you believe pain relief is needed, call your vet for an appropriate canine NSAID such as meloxicam or carprofen.

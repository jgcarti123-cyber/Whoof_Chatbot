# Canine Parvovirus (CPV-2)

## What it is

Canine parvovirus type 2 is one of the most serious infectious diseases of dogs worldwide, particularly in India where it remains highly prevalent in urban and semi-urban areas. It is an extremely hardy virus — survives in the environment for months to years and is resistant to most household disinfectants (sodium hypochlorite / bleach at 1:30 dilution is effective).

## Who is at risk

- Unvaccinated or incompletely vaccinated puppies aged 6 weeks to 6 months are at highest risk.
- Inadequately vaccinated adult dogs can be infected.
- High-risk breeds: Rottweilers, Dobermans, American Pit Bull Terriers appear to have increased susceptibility even when vaccinated.

## How it spreads

Direct contact with infected dogs or infected faeces. Indirect transmission via contaminated environments, clothing, shoes, food bowls, or leashes. The virus sheds in faeces before clinical signs appear.

## Clinical signs (onset 3-7 days after exposure)

- Severe, often haemorrhagic (bloody) diarrhoea — watery, very foul-smelling, may be yellow or grey before becoming bloody
- Projectile vomiting
- Profound lethargy and weakness
- High fever (may become hypothermic in late stages)
- Anorexia
- Rapid, severe dehydration
- Painful abdomen

**A puppy with bloody diarrhoea and vomiting should be treated as parvovirus until proven otherwise. This is an emergency — puppies can die within 24-48 hours without treatment.**

## Diagnosis

Point-of-care parvovirus ELISA antigen test (in-clinic rapid test). False negatives can occur early in infection; clinical presentation is also highly diagnostic.

## Treatment

No specific antiviral — treatment is supportive and intensive:
- IV fluids to correct dehydration and electrolyte imbalances
- Broad-spectrum antibiotics (secondary bacterial sepsis is a major killer)
- Anti-nausea medications (ondansetron, maropitant)
- Nutritional support once vomiting is controlled
- Isolation from other dogs

Survival with aggressive hospitalised care: 80-90%. Without treatment: typically fatal.

## Prevention

The DHPPi vaccine is highly effective against parvovirus. The complete puppy vaccination series is critical — do not allow puppies to contact unvaccinated dogs or potentially contaminated ground until 10-14 days after the final puppy booster. In high-risk urban areas of India, some vets recommend giving the final puppy dose at 16-18 weeks to overcome any residual maternal antibody interference.

## Disinfection

If a dog in the household has had parvovirus, disinfect all surfaces, bedding, and equipment with bleach solution (1 part bleach: 30 parts water, minimum 10-minute contact time). Dispose of porous items that cannot be bleach-disinfected.
